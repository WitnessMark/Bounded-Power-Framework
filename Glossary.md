# Glossary

Plain-language meanings of the main terms used in the Bounded Power Framework. Where a term is defined in the Core Requirements, **the Core Requirements' definition governs**; this page only summarizes it. Rule numbers refer to the Core Requirements v1.

---

## The framework

### Bounded Power Framework (BPF)
A framework for examining authority. It records what an institution's powers can do, whom they affect, what harm they can cause, and what can stop them, and compares that with a fixed set of structural limits.

### Core Requirements
The framework's rules: 101 numbered rules in seven parts (I Foundations; II The Four Floors; III Core Constraints; IV Anti-evasion; V Verification; VI Governance; VII Binding Operational). Rules are numbered Part.Section.Rule, such as 2.D.3 or 3.A.2. Published as `01-Framework/SCBP-04-Core-Requirements.md`. Only the binding text binds; the preamble, headings, "Human Understanding" passages, and closing explain but never narrow it.

### SCBP
The prefix of the framework's core documents (SCBP-01 to SCBP-09), and of record IDs (`SCBP-REG-…`). SCBP-04 is the Core Requirements; SCBP-03 is the Operations Manual, which explains how to meet them.

### The three conditions (1.1)
Power must remain small enough to stop. Harm must remain interruptible before it becomes permanent. Mistakes must remain correctable before they become irreversible. Every rule is read to serve them.

### Adopting authority (7.1)
An authority that has formally accepted the Core Requirements as binding on itself. Every other authority is non-adopting. No authority has adopted them.

### Adopted, diagnostic, and normative force
For an adopting authority, the rules bind as written. For a non-adopting authority, each rule defines a public finding, not an obligation. The Floors, and the rules of Part I and Part III-C and III-D, also state the framework's position that crossing them is illegitimate for anyone. That is a claim, not a power.

### Findings
How a record compares a power with a rule: **conforming**, **not conforming**, **compliance not demonstrated** (not enough evidence either way), or **not applicable**.

## Authority and power

### Authority
(1) The ability to compel outcomes, allocate resources, restrict behavior, or bind future action over others, whatever its intention, popularity, ownership, or legal form. (2) *An authority* is any person, body, institution, or system that holds or exercises one or more powers.

Indirect influence counts as authority when either:
- **(a) it is operationalized into compulsion** — another party relies on it as a structured part of a decision that binds identifiable people or organizations, or people cannot refuse or leave it without material cost (for example, a credit score built into loan approval); or
- **(b) its effects at scale are equivalent to directive control** — defaults, rankings, filtering, incentives, or control of information that predictably steer behavior.

Ordinary advice, reporting, teaching, and persuasion that people can and do ignore at no material cost are not authority.

### Material cost
A cost is material when avoiding an influence would mean giving up a legally protected interest, losing access to an essential good or service or a real economic opportunity, or bearing more than a minimal burden.

### Power
One specific capacity of authority: one function over defined people, things, or territory, such as issuing fines, detaining people, collecting a kind of data, scoring applicants, or setting prices. **The power, not the institution, is what gets authorized, bounded, renewed, paused, stopped, narrowed, and ended.** Records list each power separately (P1, P2, …).

### Institution
An organization or body that holds one or more powers. A record starts with the institution and then lists its powers.

### Operation
A specific activity carried out under a power.

### Minor power (3.A.4)
A low-risk power that meets every condition of 3.A.4: no force or confinement; no effect lasting beyond 30 days or resisting ordinary reversal; low-cost substitutes available (or it only allocates a voluntary benefit); spending below a set limit; and no confirmed stop, Floor finding, or successful review petition. Minor powers get a lighter process. The Floors and stop paths still apply in full, and a power loses minor status automatically when any condition fails.

### Person
A natural human being. The Floors protect persons and living systems. An organization may invoke due process (2.D.5) for its own property and legal standing, but no other Floor.

### Independent
Not funded, appointed, removable, staffed, or controlled by the authority concerned or by people who benefit from the power; not reliant on it for data or operations; and not in any exchanged arrangement with it.

## The Floors (Part II)

### Floors
Four conditions no authority may push a person or living system beneath, whatever its purpose:
- **Ecological Floor (2.A.1)** — no crossing, or moving toward, an irreversible ecological threshold.
- **Economic Floor (2.B)** — no using material need as leverage, and no debts that grow because a person cannot pay.
- **Agency Floor (2.C)** — authority must stay understandable and its protections usable by ordinary people; a person may leave.
- **Human Rights Floor (2.D)** — what authority may never do to a person: bodily integrity, limits on confinement, due process, expression, equal treatment, privacy, searches, movement, and more.

Floors do not expire, and no emergency suspends them.

### The Floors are a minimum (2.1)
Where any constitution, law, or treaty gives a person more protection against an authority, that protection also applies. No right under other law lets anyone go beneath a Floor. See `00-Reading/08-Bill-of-Rights-Crosswalk.md`.

### Protective designation (3.E.7)
A designation, supported by independent evidence, that a specific Floor condition — an ecosystem, a species, a water source, a recognized right — must be protected. The designation does not expire with the powers that enforce it. It describes what is protected; it does not itself restrict anyone. Restrictions on people belong to the enforcing powers, which keep their own terms and limits.

### Status register (2.D.12)
A record whose only purpose is to establish a person's own continuing legal status, title, or entitlement — a birth, citizenship, or enrollment record, a land title, a court judgment. It may be kept while the status lasts, used only to confirm the status, and never used for anything else.

### Accountability record (2.D.14)
A record of what an authority did: its use of force, confinement, or sanction against a person; a complaint, stop signal, or report about its conduct; discipline of its agents; or a review's outcome. Kept for 10 years in custody chosen by an independent body, available to the person concerned, oversight bodies, and courts. The authority may not destroy it.

### Sanction listing (2.D.14)
A listing or sanction against a member of the public, such as a discipline entry, a registry listing, or a blocklist entry. It is **not** an accountability record. It follows the ordinary data limits (2.D.12) and the rule against indefinite entrapment (2.D.6), and the person may ask for earlier deletion unless a hold applies.

## Before, during, and after a power

### Authorization (3.A.1, 3.A.2)
The recorded decision of an independent authorizing body that a defined power may operate, within stated limits, for a maximum term. It is never transferable, and authorizing an institution does not authorize any power it was not separately granted.

### Authorizing body (3.A.2)
Whoever decides whether a power may operate or be renewed. It must be independent of the power, able to refuse, must hear the people affected, and must decide on a public record. No authority may authorize its own powers.

### Lot panel (3.A.2, 3.A.4)
A panel chosen by lot from the people affected, excluding operators and anyone the institution pays, with appeal to an independent body. A member-governed body — a council, an owners' association, a cooperative, a congregation, a tribal nation — may have its powers authorized by a lot panel. For minor powers, a body chosen by lot or by election may authorize.

### Harm threshold (3.A.5)
A measurable limit, or an observable condition, that stops a power when crossed. Each threshold names its condition, data source, detection method, independent check, and time window. It may be tightened at any time and loosened only through re-entry.

### Term and renewal (3.E.1, 3.E.2, 7.2)
Every authorization has a maximum term (never more than 10 years; at most 2 years for high-risk or new powers). At the end of its term, a power expires unless renewed. Renewal is a new decision on current evidence; it is never automatic, and it cannot approve growth.

### Pause
An immediate, temporary halt of a power or operation, lasting until the condition that triggered it is resolved.

### Stop
A halt after a defined threshold is crossed or a trigger fact is confirmed. A stopped power keeps no authority and restarts only through re-entry.

### Re-entry
A full authorization decision, as if the power were new. It is the only way back after a stop or expiry, and the only way to loosen a limit.

### Narrowing
Reducing a power's scope, duration, reach, speed, intensity, resources, opacity, dependency, or downstream impact.

### Ending
Permanent termination of a power, including reversal of its downstream effects.

### Stop signal (3.C.3)
A report, by anyone in a position to see it, that a stop condition has occurred or that there is a credible pathway to serious harm.

### Pause, then check (3.C.3)
A stop signal reporting a defined condition pauses the operation at once. An independent checking body then decides one question only: did the trigger fact occur? If not, the pause ends. If so, the pause becomes a stop. A signal about a credible pathway to serious harm takes effect once the checking body confirms, within hours, that its facts would describe such a pathway. Giving a stop signal carries no penalty (3.C.6).

### Stop path (3.A.7, 3.C.1)
A real way to pause or stop a power that does not depend on the authority's cooperation. At least one must be held by a body the authority neither funds, appoints, nor can remove. Records describe what each stop path can actually stop:
- **whole power** — a holder independent of the institution can, under current law, halt the power entirely (for example, a court injunction against the whole program);
- **one practice** — it can stop one practice within the power;
- **one case** — it can stop the power only as applied to one person or case.

### Whole power: what does not count
A new law, a vote of the institution's own governing body, and forking or replacing the institution are not whole-power stop paths. If only a new law could stop the power as a whole, the answer is NONE EXISTS.

### Self-help is not a stop path
Paying, complying, declining, giving up, or turning a setting off may help one person, but it does not stop a power.

### Minimum essential function (3.C.5)
The smallest part of a stopped power that must keep running so that people are not pushed beneath a Floor — for example, keeping water flowing. It is run under independent control, never by the authority whose power was stopped.

### Emergency power (1.6)
An emergency never enlarges a power or disables a stop or a Floor. A power created because of an emergency expires within 90 days and may continue only through re-entry, no more than twice.

## Evidence

### Harm
Damage that reduces a person's or living system's future choice, health, safety, stability, or recoverability. It includes slow erosion as well as sudden injury, and does not require malice.

### Harm categories (3.A.5)
The kinds of harm a record must consider: bodily, psychological, economic, ecological, informational, communicative, agency, generational, and harm to non-participants.

### Causation Ladder
The evidence standard for findings that several systems are really one authority, that a variable is a prohibited proxy, or that one system's outputs are built into another's decisions. Seven tiers, strongest first: (1) formal control; (2) contractual dependency; (3) data dependency; (4) operational reliance; (5) shared decision criteria; (6) statistically shown proxy effect; (7) residual correlation. A finding needs tier 5 or stronger (tiers 1–5). Tiers 6–7 support only a recorded concern and a request for evidence. The ladder governs findings about structure; it never governs stopping.

### Three-answer rule
Every factual field in a record takes one of three answers: a **value with its source** (`[S#]`); **UNKNOWN** (you looked and could not find it); or **NONE EXISTS** (you found that it is absent). Never invent a value. NONE EXISTS is often the most important finding in a record.

### Public sources
Information anyone can obtain: laws and regulations, court decisions, government records, an institution's own publications and filings, and reputable news. Records use public sources and the preparer's own observation only.

## Records and forms

### Registration record
A structured record of one institution and its powers, made with the registration form. It lists each power, its authority basis, its stop paths, a Floor checklist, and how it compares with the Core Requirements.
- An **observed record** is made from the outside. It reports what exists and never fills in what only the institution can do.
- An **authorization record** is made by an institution that has adopted the Core Requirements, and includes its commitments: thresholds, stop paths, term, and ending plan.

### Worked example
A record of a **fictional** institution, showing how the framework applies to a kind of institution. Names, places, and figures are illustrative. v1 publishes worked examples, not records of real institutions. See `03-Examples/`.

### Witness report
A structured report of something an institution did, failed to do, or threatened to do. One form covers incidents, stop signals, challenges, change requests, and review petitions.

### Authority response
An institution's answer to a record about it: a correction, added information, a disagreement, or a notice of change. It is filed beside the record and **never replaces it**. Readers see both.

### Version
A record changes only by a new version from its original preparer. The new version quotes, word for word, every passage it changed or removed, with the reason. Earlier versions stay public.

### No silent replacement
Nothing is quietly changed or swapped out. Records change by new versions; institutions answer with responses filed beside the record; superseded framework documents stay public (6.5).

### AI bundle
A single file to give an AI assistant. You name an institution; the AI drafts a registration record following the form, the vocabulary, and the full Core Requirements. **The draft is yours.** It is not filed anywhere. If you publish it, that is your choice and your responsibility.

### Checker
A script that checks a record against the form and the vocabulary, and flags every number that has no source tag. It can confirm that a source is cited, not that the source says what the record claims.

### Vocabulary (VOCAB.yml)
The shared word lists used by the forms, the AI bundle, and the checker, so every record uses the same terms.

### Registry
In v1, the `04-Registry/` folder explains how records work and holds BPF's own record. It holds no records of other real institutions.

### WitnessMark
The name under which the Bounded Power Framework is published. WitnessMark grants no authority, issues no legal judgments, and enforces nothing.
