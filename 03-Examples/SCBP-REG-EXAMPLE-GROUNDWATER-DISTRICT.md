# [COUNTY] Groundwater Management District — Registration

## PART A — The institution

A1. **What it is, in one paragraph.** A special district that manages the aquifer under an agricultural valley. It decides how much groundwater each landowner may pump, restricts land use and new wells in a zone it has designated for protection, and fines landowners who pump more than their allocation. Its decisions decide whether farms can irrigate and, indirectly, whether household wells of people who hold no permit keep producing water.

A2. **Legal basis.** The [STATE] groundwater management law, which requires the district to adopt a management plan with minimum water levels and subsidence limits and to bring the basin into balance within 25 years [EX] of adopting the plan. The district's own enabling act gives it permit, zoning-type, and penalty powers [EX].

A3. **Who controls it.** A board of 7 directors [EX] elected by landowners in the district, one vote per acre owned [EX]. Renters and owners of household wells on small lots hold few votes.

A4. **Related entities.** The [STATE] Water Agency reviews the plan periodically [EX] and may, after a hearing, declare it inadequate and impose state pumping limits. The [STATE] Geological Survey measures subsidence with repeat surveys. All figures are the district's unless marked as the state's.

A5. **Adoption.** not_adopted.

A6. **Powers found.**
- P1 — Groundwater pumping permits and allocations.
- P2 — Restrictions in the protected recharge and subsidence zone (assigned teaching point: a protective designation, 3.E.7).
- P3 — Over-pumping penalties.
- Not recorded: well-construction permits; the household-well mitigation fund as a separate power.

**The ecological threshold (2.A.1).** The aquifer sits under layers of clay. When water levels fall far enough, the clay compacts, the land sinks, and the aquifer loses storage space that does not come back when water returns. The definition of an irreversible ecological threshold names "the loss of an aquifer" [S2]. The state survey measures sinking of up to 0.8 feet a year [EX] in one sub-area and estimates permanent storage loss so far at 1.2 million acre-feet [EX]. That evidence comes from a body independent of the district and is reproducible, as 2.A.1 requires.

**B1. What it can do to people.** Every well that pumps more than 2 acre-feet a year [EX] needs a district permit. The board sets an annual allocation per irrigated acre, 2.6 acre-feet [EX] this year, reduced by 5% a year [EX] toward an estimated sustainable 1.8 [EX]. ## Power P2 — Restrictions in the protected recharge and subsidence zone

**B1. What it can do to people.** In [EX] 2021 the board designated the clay area of worst sinking and its recharge lands as a protected zone, on state survey data. Inside the zone no new high-capacity well may be drilled, pumping is capped at 1.2 acre-feet per acre [EX], and paving more than 20% [EX] of a parcel needs district approval.

**Designation test (3.E.7).** The framework separates two things.
- The designation, which describes what is protected: the aquifer storage under the clay area. It does not restrict anyone by itself. Under 3.E.7 it should not expire, and it may be loosened only through re-entry on independent evidence that the aquifer is no longer at risk.
- The power that enforces it, P2: the restrictions on owners. That power is subject to every requirement, including a term.

Current law has it the other way round. The restrictions have no end date. The designation can be redrawn by a board majority at any meeting [EX]. Three years later [EX] the board removed 3,000 acres [EX] from the zone after landowner petitions, with no new survey data. Under 3.E.7 that was a loosening without re-entry or independent evidence.

**B2. Who and where.** Owners of about 640 parcels [EX] in the zone. Non-participants protected by it: everyone who draws from the aquifer.

## Teaching notes (non-binding)

- Aquifer compaction is a named irreversible ecological threshold [S2]. A permit system that allows pumping above recharge accumulates toward it, and a slow glide path is mitigation, not the prevention 2.A.1 requires.
- The evidence for a threshold finding must be independent and reproducible (Definitions: Irreversible ecological threshold). Here it comes from the state survey, not the district.
- A protective designation describes what is protected and should not expire; it may be loosened only through re-entry on independent evidence (3.E.7). The board's redrawing [EX] is the failure this rule targets. It is also a substantive ecological problem: the acres removed now pump without the cap, so P2's Floors finding is not conforming (2.A.1). The protective purpose of P2 does not make every exercise or relaxation of that power conforming.
- The power that enforces a designation still needs a term, thresholds, and stop paths (3.E.7, 3.E.1, 7.2). The designation lasts; P2 does not.
- A penalty below the value of what it penalizes works as a price, and pricing must never authorize crossing a threshold (2.A.1). Interest on unpaid penalties is escalation (2.B.2).
- State intervention under an existing statute is a real whole-power stop path held by an independent body. But a path never tested on this district is compliance not demonstrated (3.A.7), and its slowness matters (3.C.1).
- Household-well owners are non-participants who bear the harm (3.A.11). A remedy fund controlled by the district is not an independent remedy (3.A.10).
- Penalty revenue that funds the district is a dependency on the conduct it regulates (3.B.6).
- Reading a permitted well's meter is the inspection condition 2.D.15 allows for a licensed activity: published, and limited to its regulatory purpose. It would not reach the owner's house, papers or devices.
