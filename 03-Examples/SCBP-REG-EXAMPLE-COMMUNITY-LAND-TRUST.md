# [CITY] Community Land Trust — Registration

## PART A — The institution

A1. **What it is, in one paragraph.** A nonprofit that buys land and keeps it permanently affordable for housing. It owns the land under about 110 homes [EX]; households own the houses on it, or rent units the trust owns. Each homeowner signs a 99-year ground lease [EX] that limits the price at which the home can be resold, gives the trust a right to buy it first, requires the owner to live in it, and lets the trust end the lease for serious default. The trust's stated purpose is to keep homes affordable for later buyers, and the resale limit is how it does that.

A2. **Legal basis.** The trust's articles and bylaws; a recorded ground lease on each parcel; a [STATE] statute that expressly validates long-term affordability restrictions held by nonprofit land trusts [EX]; and, on parcels bought with city grants, an affordability covenant in favor of the [CITY] housing department.

A3. **Who controls it.** A board of 12 [EX] directors in three equal groups: leaseholders, residents of the service area who do not lease from the trust, and public representatives. The leaseholder and community groups are elected by the trust's members at the annual meeting; the public representatives are chosen by the other two groups [EX]. The board sets the formula for new leases; changing an existing lease needs the homeowner's written consent.

A4. **Related entities.** The [CITY] housing department funded about 40% of acquisitions [EX] and holds a separate recorded covenant on those parcels. [MISSION LENDER] holds acquisition loans. Figures belong to the trust unless stated.

A5. **Adoption.** not_adopted. Findings are diagnostic only [S1].

A6. **Powers found.**
- P1 — Resale-price formula and the trust's option to buy at the formula price.
- P2 — Enforcement of the ground lease, up to ending the lease and forcing a sale.
- Not recorded: admission of buyers and tenants (benefits_eligibility_or_allocation); monthly ground rent (fines_fees_or_charges).

## Power P1 — Resale-price formula and purchase option

**B1. What it can do to people.** When a homeowner sells, the price is capped by a formula: the owner's original price plus 25% of the rise in the home's appraised market value [EX], plus the value of approved improvements. The trust has 60 days [EX] to buy the home at that price or name an eligible buyer. The owner cannot sell to anyone else, at any price, unless the trust declines. Heirs may keep the home only if they meet income limits; otherwise they must sell at the formula price.

**B2. Who and where.** About 110 homeowner households [EX] and every later buyer of those homes. Heirs, children and future buyers are bound without having signed. The binding runs for 99 years [EX] and is renewable at the leaseholder's option for another 99 years [EX].

**B3. Authority basis.** `direct`. The cap and option are enforceable terms of the recorded lease.

Future generations (2.D.7): the formula binds buyers and heirs for up to 198 years [EX] with no revision point except the homeowner's consent. 2.D.7 requires long-horizon commitments to be divided into short, revisable segments. Ending a lease needs a court order under [STATE] law [EX]. The trust also has a right to cure a homeowner's mortgage default, which can prevent a bank foreclosure.

## Teaching notes (non-binding)

- The set's main 3.A.8 case. A 99-year ground lease [EX], a city covenant and a loan agreement all outlast any possible term under 7.2, and none was listed and approved individually by an independent body.
- 3.A.8 allows long financial obligations for durable infrastructure if they have revision points; it does not allow any term that restricts narrowing or ending a power. The lender clause and the city covenant both do that, so for an adopting authority they would be irreversible effects under 3.D.2.
- 2.D.7 asks for "short, revisable segments". A formula that can change only with each homeowner's consent binds heirs and later buyers for generations with no revision point, so P1's Floors row is not conforming.
- A good purpose does not change the analysis (1.4). Permanent affordability is the trust's purpose, and the record still measures the resale limit as a power.
- A path out exists (the trust must buy or find a buyer within 60 days [EX]), so 2.B.2 escalation and 2.C.3 exit are not the problem here; duration is.
- Courts do provide due process for each lease ending (2.D.5), and the trust's disclosure to buyers is a conforming example of 3.B.9.
- Members can direct the board, but they elect it, so that path is only partly independent (Definitions: Independent).
- Rule 3.E.7: a protective long-term covenant such as permanent affordability may be made a protective designation, so the protection can persist. Its enforcement mechanics, meaning the formula, the option, the covenant and the loan terms, still carry terms and revision points under 3.A.8. No designation has been made here, and the mechanics have no revision points. The term, 3.A.8 and 2.D.7 findings therefore stand; the rule now shows the route to conforming.
- A ground lease's inspection and occupancy clauses are searches of a home under 2.D.15. A standing inspection condition is allowed only for a licensed activity; under a lease, checks need individualized cause or consent freely given, so what they look at decides the finding. Here it is not shown, and P2's Floors row stays compliance not demonstrated.
