# A Reading Note on the Framework's Reasoning

*Background and context, not rules. This note explains the reasoning beneath the rules; it defines nothing. The binding text is the [Core Requirements (SCBP-04)](../../01-Framework/SCBP-04-Core-Requirements.md). Where this note seems to differ from it, the Core Requirements govern.*

---

The framework's central move is easy to miss because it is so plain: it treats power as a **material**, not as a moral problem. Everything else rests on that.

A moral account of institutional failure asks who was negligent, who was corrupt, who should have known better. The answer is often true, and almost never enough. The same failures recur across eras, ideologies, and places, under people of every kind. When a pattern survives every change of cast, the cause is unlikely to be the cast. It is more likely the structure they were all standing inside.

So the framework sets the moral question aside. Not because it doesn't matter, but because it is the wrong tool for this job. It asks a narrower, more durable question: *how does power behave once it exists, whoever holds it?* Treated as a material, power has properties you can measure, bound, and check. Naming it that way accuses no one.

## Surface area

If power is a material, it has a size. The framework measures that size in six dimensions: **scope, duration, reach, speed, intensity, and opacity** (Core Requirements 3.B.1). Power often grows along these dimensions while its formal grant stays the same. An agency keeps an emergency power past the emergency (duration). A definition stretches a little (scope). A system that touched a thousand people now touches a million (reach). A decision that took a month now takes a second (speed). Enforcement gets heavier or better funded (intensity). A process that was once clear becomes a black box (opacity). On paper, nothing changed. In effect, the power is larger.

Measuring these dimensions lets the framework see growth that a review of the formal grant would miss. Under 3.B.1, an increase in any dimension is growth, and growth without new authorization is stopped.

## Drift

Power rarely expands in one visible act. It **drifts**: it builds up through small steps, each reasonable on its own, none of which looks like the problem on the day it happens. The clinic adds one more form. The platform changes one default. The temporary measure quietly becomes permanent. No single step is the problem. The pattern is.

Drift follows from one common assumption: that **authority continues unless someone stops it.** That puts the whole burden on the people affected. They must notice, push back, and reverse what has piled up, against a system that gets harder to move every year. Almost no one can carry that burden, which is why things just continue.

The framework reverses the assumption. **Authority does not continue by default; it must justify its continuation, or it ends.** Every power expires at the end of a set term unless it is renewed on current evidence (3.E.1, 3.E.2). Nothing coasts on having always been there. The pressure moves off the people and onto the power.

## Why structure rather than morality

Choosing structure over morality is not cynicism about people. It is realism about design. A safe system should not depend on its operators being unusually careful, honest, or brave on the worst day. The working principle is blunt: **don't ask people to be better; make harm harder.** Design for ordinary human limits, not ideal behaviour. The Core Requirements say it directly: a power must stay within its limits "when operated by ordinary, inattentive, or fatigued people" (1.2). A limit that holds only while everyone is careful is not a limit. It is a hope.

This is also why the framework prefers stops to debates, defaults to discretion, and reversibility to optimization. Each preference moves safety out of judgment, where it can be argued away, and into structure, where it holds.

## What it is reaching for

From this comes a definition of safety that has nothing to do with how a system feels. A system is safe only if it can still be **stopped, examined, corrected, or replaced** without bringing everything down around it. The Core Requirements state this as three conditions (1.1): power must remain small enough to stop; harm must remain interruptible; mistakes must remain correctable. In practice that means power that is **stoppable, expiring, reversible, and visible.** A system that cannot be stopped is unsafe by this definition, even if it has never caused harm.

That definition is a premise, not a proof. The framework adopts it openly. For a fair statement of where this is open to challenge, see [Answering the Circularity Objection](../04-Assessments/Circularity-Answer.md).

There is a quieter payoff, because it is easy to mistake all this for an adversarial project. It is the opposite. When authority stays bounded, clear, renewable, and interruptible, people do not have to live in suspicion of it. Clarity reduces suspicion. Reversibility reduces fear. Expiry reduces permanence. Bounded authority generates less friction over time, not more. The goal was never to fight power. It was to keep power small enough that fighting it is rarely necessary.
