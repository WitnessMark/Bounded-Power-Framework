# Stop-Path and Protections Reference

*v1 · 2026-10-21 · Published for independent review · Not adopted by anyone*

*Reference document. Subordinate to the Core Requirements v1. It explains; it never narrows, waives, or reinterprets them (6.1).*

> **Not legal advice.** This list is **US-focused**. Names, availability, and strength vary by state and over time, and some protections are weakly enforced. That weakness is itself worth recording. For a plain-language guide to using these options, see [What You Can Do](../../00-Reading/07-What-You-Can-Do.md).

## What this is for

Most authorities that can harm people are also subject to protections: courts, oversight offices, complaint routes, laws that let citizens sue. Some of these are **stop paths** in the framework's sense. Many are not.

This reference helps you tell the difference when you fill in the stop-path table of the [Registration form](../../02-Forms/01-Registration.md) (B5), and when you describe earlier warnings and attempts to fix a problem in a [Witness Report](../../02-Forms/02-Witness-Report.md) (2e).

---

## 1. What counts as a stop path

A **stop path** is a real way to pause or stop a power that does not depend on the authority's cooperation (3.C.1). The standard, for every authority, is that the stop paths are tested, and at least one is held by a body the authority neither funds, appoints, nor can remove (3.A.7). For an authority that has not adopted the Core Requirements, a gap is a public finding, not an imposed obligation (Force and status).

For each stop path, record **what it can actually stop**:
- **whole power**: a holder independent of the institution can, **under current law**, halt the power entirely. Example: a court order against the whole program, or a takeover under an existing statute;
- **one practice**: it can stop one practice within the power, but not the power;
- **one case**: it can stop or undo the power's effect on one person or one matter.

Also record who holds it, whether that holder is independent of the institution, and your source. Whether a path reaches the whole power depends on the facts. Record what the source shows, not what the path could do in theory.

### When a legislature or other body counts

A legislature or other body counts as a whole-power stop path only if it is independent of the institution and has an existing, usable procedure that can stop the power within a defined time, for example a sunset vote that must take place, or a standing review body with power to end it. The general ability to pass a new law, or to abolish the institution, does not count.

The procedure must already exist under current law and be one that will run or can be started, for example a sunset clause under which the power ends unless a vote renews it, or a standing review body that can end the power. A referendum counts only on the same test, for example a vote on the power that current law requires by a set date. A referendum or initiative that only lets voters pass a new law is the general ability to pass a new law, and does not count. Check the holder's independence honestly.

### What does not count as a whole-power stop path

Record these as **context**, not as whole-power stop paths:
- **Elections and recalls.** Voting out officials changes who runs a power. It does not halt the power.
- **The general ability to legislate or abolish.** A legislature's general power to pass a new law, repeal the power, or abolish the institution is not a whole-power stop path. Neither is a proposed or hypothetical change in law. If that general ability is the only whole-power route, the answer is NONE EXISTS.
- **The institution's own governing body.** A vote by the council, board, or members that run the institution is not independent of it.
- **Forking or replacing the institution.** Record this as a *replacement path* in the text.

### What is not a stop path at all

**Self-help** is not a stop path: paying, complying, declining, leaving, or turning a setting off. These protect one person at their own cost. They do not stop the power. (The right to leave still matters. It is protected by 2.C.3.)

## 2. Quick map by type of authority

A starting map, not a limit. Most authorities can be reached in several ways. Check each against its source.

| Type of authority | May reach the whole power (check the facts) | Usually one practice or one case | Context, not stop paths |
|---|---|---|---|
| Police, sheriff, corrections | Court order against a whole program or practice in a civil-rights suit; receivership or takeover under an existing statute | Civil-rights suit for one person (42 U.S.C. § 1983); suppression of evidence; internal affairs; civilian oversight; federal civil-rights complaint | Elections; recall; council budget votes; public-records and open-meetings law |
| Courts | Higher court striking down a whole rule or practice | Appeal; habeas corpus; recusal; judicial-conduct commission | Judicial elections; retention votes; open-courts rules |
| Schools and school districts | State takeover or receivership under an existing statute; court order on a whole program | Special-education and student-records complaints (IDEA, FERPA); Title IX complaint; state education complaint | School-board elections; public comment |
| Child and family agencies | Court order or settlement covering a whole system | Administrative appeal; guardian ad litem or CASA; juvenile due-process rights | Elections; legislative hearings |
| Health and hospitals | License suspension by a licensing body; exclusion from a federal program | Licensing-board complaint; federal health-privacy complaint (HIPAA); malpractice claim; ethics-board review of research | The hospital's own board |
| Tax, revenue, benefits | Court ruling that a whole rule or program is unlawful | Administrative appeal; ombudsman or taxpayer advocate; inspector-general complaint | Elections; the general power to change the law |
| Environment and utilities | Citizen suit for an injunction under an environmental statute; permit revocation by a regulator | Agency complaint; public-utility commission complaint; permit challenge | Public comment on environmental review (NEPA); elections |
| Executive offices and legislatures | Court ruling that an action is unlawful | Inspector general; auditor; public-records request | Elections; recall; referendum or initiative (unless current law provides a binding one that meets the test in section 1) |
| Private entities exercising public power | Sector regulator order; court injunction (consumer, contract, or antitrust law) | Attorney-general or consumer complaint; individual or class lawsuit | The company's own board; shareholder votes; leaving the service (self-help) |

Public-records and open-meetings laws stop nothing. They are still among the most useful tools: they show what the power does and who holds the real stop paths.

## 3. Protections for you as a recorder

These protect people who record and report. They are not stop paths.

- **First Amendment:** speech, press, and the right to petition government.
- **Government bodies cannot sue for defamation.** Individual officials can, but truth and honest opinion are defenses.
- **Anti-SLAPP laws** in many states let you get a lawsuit filed to silence you dismissed quickly, and recover costs.
- **Public-records (FOIA) and open-meetings laws** give you access to public information.
- **Section 230** limits a host's liability for content others post. It matters only if you publish others' reports.

Follow the record rules anyway: public sources only, people by role not by name, and UNKNOWN instead of a guess (SCBP-03 Step 0.5).

## 4. Protections for people harmed by authorities

**Constitutional rights:** due process (5th and 14th Amendments), equal protection, protection from unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment), and the rights of speech, assembly, and petition (1st Amendment).

**Ways to hold authorities to account:** civil-rights suits against state and local officials (42 U.S.C. § 1983) and, in narrow cases, federal officers; federal and state tort-claims acts; civil-rights statutes (Title VI and VII, the ADA, the Fair Housing Act); class actions; judicial review; habeas corpus.

**Oversight and disclosure:** inspectors general, ombudsman offices, auditors; whistleblower protections and the False Claims Act; agency complaint processes.

Most of these reach one case or one practice. A class action, or a suit asking for an order against a whole program, may reach the whole power. Record what the source shows.

## 5. Protections for children

Mandatory-reporting laws and child-protection oversight; the federal child-abuse prevention law (CAPTA); special-education and student-records laws (IDEA, FERPA); Title IX; due-process rights for young people in juvenile court, recognized by the US Supreme Court in 1967; guardians ad litem and CASA volunteers; the children's online-privacy law (COPPA); the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (signed but not ratified by the US).

## 6. Protections for the environment

Environmental review with public comment (NEPA); the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, Safe Drinking Water Act, and hazardous-waste laws (RCRA, CERCLA); the Endangered Species Act; **citizen-suit provisions**, which let citizens sue a violator, or an agency that fails to enforce, directly; state environmental agencies; environmental-justice review; the public-trust doctrine.

Citizen suits and permit revocations can sometimes stop a whole operation. Public comment is context, not a stop path.

## 7. Why this matters

Each item above is a **designed** protection. The question a record asks is whether it is **real, reachable, and effective**, or only on paper. A protection that exists but cannot be used is the kind of quiet failure the framework is meant to show (see pattern 8 in the [Structural Failure Casebook](Structural-Failure-Casebook.md)).

The most important line in many records is the honest answer to one question: *Can anything stop this power as a whole?* If the answer is **NONE EXISTS**, say so. That is a finding, not a failure of the record (SCBP-03 Step 0.4).
