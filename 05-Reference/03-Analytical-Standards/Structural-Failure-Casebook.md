# Structural Failure Casebook

*v1 · 2026-10-21 · Published for independent review · Not adopted by anyone*

*Guidance document. Subordinate to the Core Requirements v1. It may explain them; it may never narrow, waive, or reinterpret them (6.1).*

---

## What this is for

This casebook describes common ways that authority slips out of its limits. Each pattern lists the signs to watch for and the rules that are meant to prevent it.

The illustrations are **generic**. They describe kinds of events that have happened in many places and times. They do not describe, and are not findings about, any real institution or person.

Rule 7.4 requires each authority to keep a record of known structural failure modes and review it periodically. This casebook can be a starting list. Recording a failure never permits it to continue (7.4).

---

## 1. Emergency creep

**Pattern:** a temporary emergency power outlives the emergency, or spreads beyond it.

**Illustration:** a measure adopted for one crisis is renewed every year for decades, and is later used for matters unrelated to that crisis.

**Signs:**
- repeated extensions, each with a slightly new justification;
- less scrutiny at each extension;
- a narrow threat redefined as a broad category.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- an emergency enlarges no power; an emergency power expires within 90 days and can continue only through re-entry, at most twice, each time for less (1.6);
- every power expires unless renewed (3.E.1);
- growth in any dimension is stopped (3.B.1);
- when the founding assumptions no longer hold, the power ends (3.E.4).

---

## 2. Entrenchment

**Pattern:** a body gathers discretion over its own mandate and resists ending.

**Illustration:** a commission set up for a five-year task still exists thirty years later, with a larger budget and a role it defined for itself.

**Signs:**
- renewal becomes automatic;
- oversight becomes a formality;
- the budget grows while the scope is said to be unchanged.

**Structural failure:** scope looks constant on paper while practical reach grows.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- no authority interprets its own scope (Interpretation; 7.3);
- outcomes, not labels, decide what a power does (5.4);
- renewal compares current operation with the original grant (3.E.2), and powers must shrink over time unless independent evidence shows otherwise (3.E.3);
- no body decides more than two renewals in a row of the same power (3.A.2);
- no power may become impractical to remove (3.B.7).

---

## 3. Information monopoly

**Pattern:** information about the power, or the people it affects, gathers in the hands of the authority or its partners.

**Illustration:** a records system built for one program becomes the data source for many others, and only its operator can check whether its outputs are right.

**Signs:**
- data combined across domains;
- outside auditors refused access;
- no one else can reproduce the results.

**Structural failure:** challenge becomes impossible without any open use of force.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- combining data across domains is a new power needing its own authorization (3.B.3);
- derived outputs inherit the source's limits (4.4);
- independent reviewers keep access (5.1), and decision inputs are declared (5.3);
- thresholds may not depend on anything the authority controls (3.A.5);
- personal information is limited and deleted when its purpose ends (2.D.12).

---

## 4. Vendor lock-in

**Pattern:** a technical system becomes impossible to replace.

**Illustration:** a public body renews its case-management software without competition, term after term, because no one else can read the data.

**Signs:**
- switching costs rise each term;
- contracts renew themselves;
- the system is woven into other systems.

**Structural failure:** reversal exists on paper only.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- no arrangement may renew itself, carry an exit penalty, or lock data in a format that cannot move; replacement must stay feasible within one term (3.A.8);
- no authority may build advantages that make it harder to replace (3.B.7);
- dependencies that threaten independence must be disclosed (3.B.6);
- a necessary function must not depend on a single structure (3.B.2).

---

## 5. Renewal as ritual

**Pattern:** renewal stops being a review of evidence and becomes a formality.

**Illustration:** a renewal board approves every application at one meeting, with a short form and no evidence.

**Signs:**
- renewal documents get shorter;
- fewer independent challenges;
- approval becomes predictable, and faster.

**Structural failure:** expiration no longer means anything.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- renewal requires evidence on every point of 3.E.2 and 3.E.3; without recorded findings it is void (3.A.2);
- faster approvals without more evidence, and fewer outside auditors, require expanded independent review (7.7);
- see the [Renewal Evidence Standard](Renewal-Evidence-Standard.md).

## 6. Capture through funding

**Pattern:** an oversight body depends on the people it oversees.

**Illustration:** an inspector's budget depends directly on payments controlled by the entities it inspects. Those entities can reduce or withdraw the funding when inspections become inconvenient. Staff also move between the inspector and the regulated entities without a cooling-off period.

**Signs:**
- budget depends on fees from those regulated;
- staff move between overseer and overseen with no waiting period;
- reluctance to stop anything because of the economic effect.

**Structural failure:** the stop holder can no longer afford to stop.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- "independent" excludes bodies funded, appointed, staffed, or controlled by the authority, and requires a cooling-off period (Definitions: Independent);
- oversight bodies are independent, and cutting their resources is retaliation (3.A.3, 3.C.6);
- at least one stop path is held by a body the authority neither funds, appoints, nor can remove (3.A.7);
- institutions may not become dependent on concentrated private interests (3.B.6).

## 7. Harm reviewed only by those causing it

**Pattern:** a harm continues for years because the only people who review it are the people responsible for it.

**Illustration:** a long-running public health study withheld known treatment from its participants for decades. Internal reviews let it continue. It ended only after an insider made it public.

**Signs:**
- the people harmed are not told what is being done to them;
- the only review is internal;
- concerns raised inside go nowhere, or cost the person who raised them.

**Structural failure:** no one outside the authority can see the harm or stop it.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- no one may be subjected to irreversible bodily harm, or to involuntary medical procedures as coercion (2.D.1);
- people must be told, without asking, what is being done to them and how to challenge it (2.C.1);
- anyone positioned to see a stop condition may give a stop signal, and it pauses the operation (3.C.3);
- no penalty or retaliation for signaling or reporting (3.C.6);
- thresholds may not depend on the authority's own findings (3.A.5);
- repeated Floor violations are a structural finding, not a series of incidents (3.D.5).

---

## 8. A protection on paper only

**Pattern:** a stop path or complaint route exists, but cannot actually be used.

**Illustration:** a complaint route sends every complaint back to the office complained about, has a short deadline, and requires a lawyer to file.

**Signs:**
- the route is controlled by the authority it is meant to check;
- using it needs forms, proof, money, or expertise;
- it can stop one person's case, but never the power itself.

**Structural failure:** the power looks stoppable, but is not.

**Rules that prevent it:**
- protections must be usable by ordinary people, and reporting needs no form, proof, or fee (2.C.2);
- stop paths must work without the authority's cooperation, and be tested (3.A.7, 3.C.1);
- see the [Stop-Path and Protections Reference](Stop-Path-Protections-Reference.md) on what counts as a whole-power stop path.

## Common threads

Across these patterns:
- change is gradual, not sudden;
- complexity hides growth (1.7);
- dependencies pile up;
- visibility falls over time (3.B.9);
- permanence comes to feel normal.

Bounded power depends on catching these early, by routine, not in a crisis. When boundaries are visible, powers can be reversed, and anyone can challenge them, most of these failures never need to become conflicts.
