---

The [Orientation](SCBP-01-Orientation.md) makes the case: power drifts because continuation is the default, safety means staying stoppable, and authority must justify its continuation or end. This document does not repeat that. It unpacks three mechanics the Orientation only points at — how to tell authority from the work it directs, how authority evades limits by splitting up, and how information quietly becomes authority of its own — and ends with the working vocabulary the rest of the framework uses.

---

## Authority is not the work it directs

Two things get treated as one, and keeping them apart is the start of everything else.

**Authority** is the power to decide. **Structure** is everything that carries the decision out — people, processes, contractors, software, paperwork. A system can have a thousand moving parts, and none of them is the source of its authority; they execute what authority directs. The reason this matters is simple: responsibility does not slide down to the hands doing the task. Spread the work across as many layers as you like — when the system causes harm, the authority that directed it still answers. **Work can be distributed. Responsibility cannot.** This is the framework's rule, not a description of how organizations behave: responsibility often does spread out in practice, and that is exactly what lets harm escape accountability. The framework refuses to follow it there. It assigns responsibility to the directing authority by design (Core Requirements 4.1).

Authority also comes in pieces. An institution usually holds several separate **powers** — issuing fines, detaining people, collecting a kind of data, scoring applicants. The framework treats each power as its own unit: each one is authorized, bounded, renewed, paused, stopped, and ended on its own, and the institution answers for each (3.A.1).

### The kitchen

Picture a restaurant kitchen. The line cooks chop, sear, and plate — that is the **structure**, the work being carried out. The owner decides what the kitchen does, how it runs, and on whose terms — that is the **authority**. If the kitchen poisons someone, it is the owner who answers, not the cook who held the pan. Responsibility sits with the one who decides.

Now add the health inspector. The inspector does not cook and does not run the kitchen — the inspector can *close* it. That is a third thing again: not the authority over the kitchen, and not its structure, but the **stop mechanism** — the power to halt, held by someone outside, beholden to no one inside.

A safe kitchen needs all three kept distinct: someone who decides and is accountable, hands that carry it out, and an outsider who can shut it down. The common mistake is to confuse the inspector for the authority — to treat the power to *stop* a thing as if it were the power to *run* it. They are opposites. Blur them and you lose the very separation that keeps the kitchen safe: the stopper starts running things, and no one is left outside to pull the cord.

## Fragmentation: evading limits by splitting up

A system that would exceed its limits as one body can try to slip them by breaking into many. Authority is divided across agencies, departments, contractors, or subsidiaries, and each piece looks modest on its own. Taken together, they act as a single system — and the combined effect is what matters.

The rule, then, attaches to the whole, not the pieces. If the parts work together as one function, they are treated as one authority for every limit, no matter how many names are on the doors (4.2). But a claim that separate bodies are "really one" has to be shown, not assumed: the Causation Ladder in the Core Requirements sets how strong the evidence must be. Similar outcomes alone are not enough.

The [Anti-Evasion Map](SCBP-04-Anti-Evasion-Map.md) shows splitting up as one of seven ways of evading a limit, and the rules that close each.

## Information as authority

In modern systems, authority is often exercised through information rather than direct command. Data systems store, sort, and act on records about people — and when those records persist indefinitely, they can become a kind of authority that works through memory instead of action. A person can be permanently defined by information that never expires and that they cannot correct or remove.

What makes this authority is not that the information influences — much information influences — but that the person cannot escape it: others act on the record to grant or deny, and the person can neither correct it nor get out of its reach without real cost. That inescapability is the compulsion. Where information can be freely disregarded, or its subject can walk away without material cost, it influences but does not govern. Where it does govern, holding it is a power like any other, and it must be bounded, visible, and able to expire. Separately, every authority's keeping of personal information has limits of its own: it must be deleted when its purpose ends and, apart from a few narrow exceptions, kept no more than 20 years (2.D.12).

This is a deliberate widening of the plain picture above — "authority is the power to decide" — to reach systems that decide nothing yet compel through inescapability. It is an expansion, not a restatement. The Authority definition in the Core Requirements sets the exact point at which influence becomes authority.

## Working terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Authority** | The power to decide or compel an outcome. |
| **Power** | One specific function of authority, such as issuing fines or scoring applicants. The unit that is authorized, stopped, and ended. |
| **Structure** | The system that carries decisions out. |
| **Control** | The ability to stop, examine, correct, or remove a system. |
| **Stop mechanism** | An outside power that can halt a system without running it. |
| **Drift** | Gradual expansion past original limits, one small step at a time. |
| **Fragmentation** | Splitting authority into parts that each look limited but act as one. |
| **Reversibility** | The ability to safely undo or replace. |
| **Scope** | The defined boundary of a power. |
| **Dependency** | When other systems rely on a system to function. |

These are plain-language summaries. Where the Core Requirements define a term, their definition governs.
