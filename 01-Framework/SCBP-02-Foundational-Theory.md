# SCBP-02 — Foundational Theory

*v1 · 2026-11-05 · Published for independent review · Not adopted by anyone*

*The mechanics beneath the rules. Explanatory, not binding: where this document and the [Core Requirements (SCBP-04)](SCBP-04-Core-Requirements.md) differ, the Core Requirements govern.*

This document sets out the ideas the Core Requirements rest on: power treated as a material with a size; drift, and the inversion that answers it; the separation of deciding, doing and stopping; evasion by splitting up; and information that becomes authority. It ends with the working terms the rest of the framework uses. The reasoning in full is in [BPF Reasoning](../00-Reading/3-Understand-the-Framework/BPF-Reasoning.md).

## Power as a material

The framework treats power as a material, not as a moral problem. Everything else rests on that choice.

A moral account of institutional failure asks who was negligent, who was corrupt, who should have known better. The answer is often true, and seldom sufficient. The same failures recur across eras, ideologies and places, under people of every kind. When a pattern survives every change of cast, its cause is unlikely to be the cast. It is more likely the structure all of them stood inside.

The framework therefore sets the moral question aside. It does not do so because the question is unimportant, but because it is the wrong instrument for this task. It asks a narrower and more durable question: how does power behave once it exists, whoever holds it? Treated as a material, power has properties that can be measured, bounded and checked. Naming it this way accuses no one.

## Surface area

A material has a size. The framework measures the size of a power in six dimensions: **scope, duration, reach, speed, intensity and opacity** (3.B.1). A power tends to grow along these dimensions while its formal grant stays the same. An emergency power is kept after the emergency (duration). A definition stretches (scope). A system that reached a thousand people reaches a million (reach). A decision that took a month takes a second (speed). Enforcement becomes heavier or better funded (intensity). A process that was once clear becomes a black box (opacity). On paper nothing has changed. In effect, the power is larger.

Measuring these dimensions shows growth that a review of the formal grant would miss. Under 3.B.1, an increase in any dimension is growth, and growth without new authorization is stopped.

## Drift, and the inversion

Power seldom expands in one visible act. It **drifts**: it accumulates through small steps, each reasonable on its own, none of which looks like the problem on the day it happens. A clinic adds one more form. A platform changes one default. A temporary measure becomes permanent. No single step is the problem. The pattern is.

Drift follows from an assumption so common it is rarely examined: *authority continues unless someone stops it.* That places the whole burden on the people affected. They must notice, object, and reverse what has accumulated, against a system that becomes harder to move every year. Few can carry that burden, which is why things continue.

The framework reverses the assumption. Authority does not continue by default. It must justify its continuation, and what cannot be justified ends. Every power is authorized for a set term and expires at the end of it unless it is renewed on current evidence (3.E.1, 3.E.2).

### What the inversion does not mean

The inversion does not mean that institutions shut down. It applies to each power separately, and a power that is still justified is renewed and continues without interruption. A worked case shows how.

A school district holds several separate powers over students: to suspend them, to search their belongings under set conditions, to impose discipline. Each is authorized, reviewed and renewed on its own (3.A.1).

The power to suspend is authorized for a set term (3.E.1, 7.2). Before the term ends, an independent body decides whether to renew it, on evidence (3.A.2, 3.E.2): whether students were treated fairly, whether mistakes were corrected, whether suspensions caused avoidable harm, whether students could challenge them, and whether a body outside the district could still stop the power. The review also compares how the power operates now with what was first granted. Growth beyond the grant cannot be renewed; it needs a new authorization.

- **If the evidence supports it,** the power is renewed and continues without interruption.
- **If it does not,** renewal is refused, and the power stops.
- **If the term runs out before renewal is decided,** the power stops until it is authorized again through re-entry (3.E.1).

In each case only that power is affected. Teachers keep teaching, the buses keep running, and the school stays open. Where stopping a power would push people beneath a Floor, its minimum essential function continues (3.C.5).

The district does not decide its own renewal. Those who use a power are not those who decide whether they keep it.

The framework does not require institutions to close and begin again every few years. It requires each power to show, at set intervals, that it is still justified, and that it has been narrowed where it can be, or why it cannot be (3.E.3). A power that meets that standard continues. One that does not cannot continue merely because it has always been there.

*The case illustrates the Core Requirements, not the rules that govern public schools today.* A fictional [school district record](../03-Examples/SCBP-REG-EXAMPLE-SCHOOL-DISTRICT.md) and a worked [renewal review](../03-Examples/Renewal-Workflow/Renewal-Review-EXAMPLE.md) show the same ideas in the framework's forms.

## Deciding, doing and stopping

Three roles are kept apart, and much of the framework depends on keeping them apart.

**Authority** is the power to decide. **Structure** is everything that carries the decision out: people, processes, contractors, software, paperwork. A system may have a thousand working parts, and none of them is the source of its authority; they carry out what authority directs. Responsibility does not move down to the hands doing the task. However many layers the work passes through, when the system causes harm, the authority that directed it answers for it. *Work can be distributed. Responsibility cannot* (4.1). This is the framework's rule, not a description of how organizations behave. In practice responsibility tends to disperse, and that dispersal is what lets harm escape accountability. The framework assigns responsibility to the directing authority by design.

The third role is the **stop**: the power to halt a power, held outside the institution, by someone it neither appoints, funds nor can remove (3.A.7, 3.C.1). A health inspector does not run a restaurant and does not cook in it, yet can close it. The holder of a stop exercises authority only for that narrow function (3.A.3). The power to halt a thing is not the power to run it, and each role stays in check only while the three remain separate.

Authority also comes in pieces. An institution usually holds several separate **powers**: issuing fines, detaining people, collecting a kind of data, scoring applicants. Each power is a unit of its own. It is authorized, bounded, renewed, paused, stopped and ended on its own, and the institution answers for each (3.A.1).

## Fragmentation: evading limits by splitting up

A system that would exceed its limits as one body can try to escape them by dividing into many. Authority is spread across agencies, departments, contractors or subsidiaries, and each piece looks modest on its own. Together they act as one system, and the combined effect is what matters.

The rule therefore attaches to the whole, not to the pieces. Parts that work together as one function are treated as one authority for every limit, however many names are on the doors (4.2). A claim that separate bodies are in fact one must be shown, not assumed: the Causation Ladder in the Core Requirements sets how strong the evidence must be. Similar outcomes alone are not enough.

The [Anti-Evasion Map](SCBP-04-Anti-Evasion-Map.md) shows splitting up as one of seven ways of evading a limit, and the rules that close each.

## Information as authority

Authority is often exercised through information rather than direct command. Data systems store, sort and act on records about people, and records that persist indefinitely can become a form of authority that works through memory instead of action. A person can be defined, permanently, by information that never expires and that they can neither correct nor remove.

What makes this authority is not that the information has influence, since much information does, but that the person cannot escape it: others act on the record to grant or to deny, and the person can neither correct it nor leave its reach without real cost. That inescapability is the compulsion. Where information can be freely disregarded, or its subject can walk away without material cost, it influences but does not govern. Where it governs, holding it is a power like any other, and it must be bounded, visible and able to expire. Separately, every authority's keeping of personal information has limits of its own: it is deleted when its purpose ends and, apart from a few narrow exceptions, kept no longer than 20 years (2.D.12).

This deliberately widens the plain definition above, authority as the power to decide, to reach systems that decide nothing yet compel through inescapability. The definition of Authority in the Core Requirements sets the exact point at which influence becomes authority.

## Why structure rather than morality

Choosing structure over morality is not cynicism about people. It is realism about design. A safe system does not depend on its operators being unusually careful, honest or brave on the worst day. The working principle is plain: *do not ask people to be better; make harm harder.* The Core Requirements say it directly: a power must remain within its limits when operated by ordinary, inattentive or fatigued people (1.2). A limit that holds only while everyone is careful is not a limit.

The same reasoning explains the framework's preferences: stops over debates, defaults over discretion, reversibility over optimization. Each moves safety out of judgment, where it can be argued away, and into structure, where it holds.

From this follows a definition of safety that has nothing to do with how a system feels. A system is safe only if it can still be **stopped, examined, corrected or replaced** without bringing everything down with it. The Core Requirements state this as three conditions (1.1): power remains small enough to stop; harm remains interruptible; mistakes remain correctable. A system that cannot be stopped is unsafe by this definition, even if it has never caused harm. The definition is a premise, adopted openly, not a proof; [Answering the Circularity Objection](../05-Reference/02-Assessments/Circularity-Answer.md) sets out where it is open to challenge.

Bounded authority is not an adversarial idea. When authority stays bounded, clear, renewable and interruptible, people have less reason to live in suspicion of it. Clarity reduces suspicion. Reversibility reduces fear. Expiry reduces permanence. The aim is not to fight power. It is to keep power small enough that fighting it is rarely necessary.

## Working terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Authority** | The power to decide or compel an outcome. |
| **Power** | One specific function of authority, such as issuing fines or scoring applicants. The unit that is authorized, renewed, stopped and ended. |
| **Structure** | The system that carries decisions out. |
| **Stop** | A power, held outside the institution, to halt a power without running it. |
| **Control** | The ability to stop, examine, correct or remove a system. |
| **Surface area** | The size of a power: its scope, duration, reach, speed, intensity and opacity. |
| **Drift** | Gradual expansion past original limits, one small step at a time. |
| **Fragmentation** | Splitting authority into parts that each look limited but act as one. |
| **Reversibility** | The ability to undo or replace safely. |
| **Dependency** | The reliance of other systems on a system in order to function. |

These are plain-language summaries. Where the Core Requirements define a term, their definition governs.
