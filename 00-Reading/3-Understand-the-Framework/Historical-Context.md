# Historical Context

This is background reading, not part of the framework's rules. Nothing here is a record or a finding about any institution. It shows what the framework's core idea looks like in widely reported history, and points to the underlying data for anyone who wants it.

The framework rests on three conditions (Core Requirements 1.1): power must remain small enough to stop, harm must remain interruptible, and mistakes must remain correctable. In practice, four properties keep power bounded:

- it can be **stopped** by those it affects (3.A.7, 3.C.1);
- its permission **expires** rather than continuing by momentum (3.E.1);
- its decisions can be **reversed** (3.D.1);
- its existence and limits are **visible** to the people it affects (3.B.9).

Power drifts when one of these lapses. The pattern below is not a moral claim about any era. It is the same structural failure recurring in different forms.

---

## When stoppability lapses

The people who bear a cost have no way to halt it. In the 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist factory fire, workers could not escape a risk imposed on them: the doors were locked, and no outside safety floor applied. The harm was not a failure of intent. It was the absence of any stop the people affected could reach.

## When expiration lapses

A program continues by momentum, its justification never re-examined. The Tuskegee syphilis study ran for forty years and continued even after a standard treatment existed. Nothing about it had to be renewed or re-justified to keep going. It simply continued until public exposure ended it.

## When reversibility lapses

A decision cannot be undone once made. Tetraethyl lead was added to gasoline for a whole population. Once dispersed, the exposure could not be recalled, and the harm built up for decades before the phase-out. The framework prefers designs where harm can be undone, because repair after the fact is uncertain and often impossible.

## When visibility lapses

An operation cannot be seen by those it affects. The MKUltra program experimented on people who had not consented, and many of its records were later destroyed. Those harmed could not learn what had been done or seek redress. Hidden power is harder to interrupt. That is why the framework requires the existence and limits of every power to be knowable.

## When evidence comes from the interested party

A recurring pattern runs through the cases: the party that profits from a practice supplies the evidence on whether the practice is safe, and that evidence prevails over independent expert warning.

After tetraethyl lead was added to gasoline, the research on whether everyday exposure caused harm was, for about four decades, funded largely by the industry that sold it, and it found no cause for concern. Independent researchers who disputed it met resistance, and the phase-out came only after their findings could no longer be set aside. Tobacco companies, from the 1950s, funded research and public messaging that cast doubt on the link between smoking and disease. In 1994 their chief executives told Congress they did not believe nicotine was addictive; internal documents later showed the industry had known otherwise.

In both cases the harm was not hidden for want of evidence. The independent evidence existed. It was outweighed by evidence from the party with the most to lose. The Core Requirements answer this directly: where evidence of harm is disputed, independently obtained evidence takes priority, and any decision to discount it must give its reasons on the public record (3.A.5). Evidence that is internally dependent or selectively disclosed is invalid for authorization, renewal or compliance (5.2), and only bodies independent of the authority count as external validation (Definitions).

## When the failure is finally visible

A separate question follows: when a failure becomes visible, does any consequence reach the people who ran it? The record is uneven. After the savings-and-loan crisis and the frauds of the early 2000s, some individuals were convicted and imprisoned. After the 2008 financial crisis and in many later corporate cases, large institutions paid penalties while few or no senior decision-makers were imprisoned.

This is the gap the framework is built around. It relies on structural limits set *before* harm — stop paths, expiry, reversibility, visibility — rather than on accountability arriving afterward, because afterward it often does not.

Each row of the US list cites one or two public sources; check the cited original before relying on any figure (see the [data README](../../05-Reference/04-Supporting-Data/README.md)).

## Caveats

- **Context, not rules.** Nothing here is a record or a finding. Removing this page would not change how the framework works.
- **Illustrative, not complete.** One or two examples are given per pattern. The datasets hold many more, and most events show more than one pattern.
- **Simplified.** Outcomes are compressed, and some cases may still be under appeal or litigation. To report an error, see [TERMS](../../TERMS.md).
