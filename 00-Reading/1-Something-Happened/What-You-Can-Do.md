# What You Can Do

*The usual options when an authority, whether a government office, an agency, a company or another institution, causes harm. For each: what it is, when it fits, how it starts, and what follows. A longer list is in the [Stop-Path and Protections Reference](../../05-Reference/01-Analytical-Standards/Stop-Path-Protections-Reference.md).*

> This is general information, not legal advice, and it promises no outcome. The options are mostly those of United States law. Rules and deadlines differ by state and change over time, and many options have **deadlines**. Where the harm is serious, a short consultation with a lawyer or a legal aid office is the safest first step.
>
> **This framework replaces none of these options.** No institution has adopted the Core Requirements, so using the framework does not by itself pause, stop or change anything. Rights under law apply in full (Core Requirements 2.1).

## Three questions

1. **Is the aim mainly a record?** A **witness report** (this framework) and a **public-records request**.
2. **Is the aim an official investigation?** A **complaint** to an oversight body: an inspector general, an ombudsman, or the relevant agency.
3. **Is the aim a remedy for personal harm?** An **administrative complaint**, **small claims**, or a conversation with a lawyer about a **civil-rights or civil suit**.

These are not exclusive. A written record costs little, and it tends to strengthen every other step.

## The options

### 1. Public-records request
**What it is:** The legal right to ask a government body for its records: emails, reports, data, policies. Federal agencies are covered by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA); each state has its own public-records law.
**When it fits:** Something is suspected but not documented, or the question is what an authority actually did or decided.
**How it starts:** A written request, often a short email, sent through the agency's "public records" or "FOIA" page, describing the records sought.
**What follows:** A response within a period set by law, which varies. Some records may be withheld, with a stated reason that can be challenged. Usually free or low-cost.

### 2. Witness report (this framework)
**What it is:** A structured, dated account of what an institution did, failed to do or threatened to do. See the [Witness Report Guide](Witness-Report-Guide.md), or the [AI Witness Interview](AI-Witness-Interview-Bundle.md).
**When it fits:** A clear, dated record is wanted, especially where the institution's own complaint channel leads nowhere. It also prepares the ground for a complaint, a records request or a lawyer.
**How it starts:** The guide, the web form or the AI interview. A signed statement in time order, with the author's own words kept beside it. Public sources and first-hand observation only; names, numbers and descriptions kept as the author gives them; UNKNOWN for what is not known. A separate copy for sharing, which identifies no private person, can be made if wanted.
**What follows:** *The report belongs to its author.* This framework has no place to file it, and writing it starts no official process. Keeping it, sharing it or attaching it to a complaint is the author's decision, as is publishing it, which is also the author's responsibility. See [TERMS](../../TERMS.md).

### 3. Complaint to an oversight body
**What it is:** An internal watchdog (an inspector general) or an independent ombudsman, whose task is to investigate misconduct, waste or unfair treatment.
**When it fits:** Misuse of authority, waste, retaliation, or a pattern that warrants investigation.
**How it starts:** A search for "[agency name] inspector general complaint" or "[state] ombudsman". Most have an online form.
**What follows:** An investigation, a referral or a refusal. Many can keep the complainant's identity confidential. No cost.

### 4. Administrative complaint
**What it is:** A complaint system for a specific kind of harm: discrimination (federal and state civil-rights offices), housing (the federal housing department), workplace safety (the federal workplace-safety agency), consumer problems (the state attorney general or the federal consumer financial regulator).
**When it fits:** The harm falls in a category one of these offices handles.
**How it starts:** A search for "[type of harm] complaint [state]", for example "housing discrimination complaint [state]".
**What follows:** Intake, and possibly an investigation or mediation. These often have **strict deadlines**, sometimes 180 days. Usually free.

### 5. Elected representatives, public comment, open meetings
**What it is:** Council members, state legislators and members of Congress keep staff for casework: helping constituents stuck with an agency. Public bodies must also allow people to speak at meetings and to comment on proposed rules.
**When it fits:** An agency is not responding, or a decision is not yet final.
**How it starts:** The representative's office, usually through a help form, or the relevant public meeting or comment period.
**What follows:** Casework often resolves matters quietly. Public comment creates a record that decision-makers must consider. Free.

### 6. Small claims court
**What it is:** A simplified court for modest money disputes, designed to be used **without a lawyer**.
**When it fits:** A specific, limited financial harm.
**How it starts:** The county court's website, which lists the dollar limit and the forms.
**What follows:** A low filing fee, a fairly quick hearing, and a case presented in person.

### 7. Civil-rights claim or civil lawsuit
**What it is:** A suit under federal civil-rights law (42 U.S.C. § 1983) where a government official violated constitutional rights, or an ordinary civil suit for other harms.
**When it fits:** Serious harm: wrongful detention, excessive force, denial of due process, discrimination.
**How it starts:** A civil-rights lawyer or a legal aid office. Many offer a free consultation, and many take strong cases on contingency, with no fee in advance.
**What follows:** A slower, more involved process, with real remedies: damages and court orders. **Deadlines apply.**

### 8. Class action
**What it is:** One lawsuit on behalf of many people harmed in the same way.
**When it fits:** The harm is small for each person but reaches many.
**How it starts:** A class-action firm or a legal aid organization evaluates it.
**What follows:** A long process, and the usual route for widespread harms where no single loss justifies a suit of its own.

### 9. Whistleblower protections and the False Claims Act
**What it is:** Protection, and sometimes a reward, for people who report fraud or wrongdoing from inside an organization. The False Claims Act ("qui tam") lets an insider sue over fraud against the government.
**When it fits:** Inside knowledge of fraud, waste or illegal conduct.
**How it starts:** A whistleblower lawyer, consulted *before* reporting: how a report is made affects the protection it receives.
**What follows:** Strong protection against retaliation, when the procedure is followed.

### 10. Anti-SLAPP: when speaking up draws a lawsuit
**What it is:** In many states, a law that allows a lawsuit filed to silence public criticism to be dismissed quickly, with costs recovered.
**When it fits:** An authority or a company sues, or threatens to sue, over a good-faith public statement or report.
**How it starts:** A lawyer, told at once that the suit appears to be a SLAPP, and asked whether the state's anti-SLAPP law applies.
**What follows:** Where the law applies, a fast-track dismissal and possible recovery of fees.

## When the person harmed is a child

- **Child protective services.** Anyone may report suspected abuse or neglect to child protective services or a child-abuse hotline; some people are required to.
- **Special education and school records.** Under federal law (IDEA and FERPA), children with disabilities have enforceable rights to services, and families can obtain records and dispute decisions.
- **Title IX.** Protection against sex discrimination and harassment in schools.
- **Guardian ad litem or court-appointed advocate.** A court can appoint an independent advocate for a child's interests.

## When the harm is to the environment

- **Citizen suits.** Most major federal environmental laws, including the Clean Air Act and the Clean Water Act, allow any person to sue a violator, or an agency that fails to enforce the law. The tool is powerful and little known.
- **Environmental agency complaints.** Pollution and permit violations can be reported to the federal or state environmental agency.
- **Comment on permits and projects.** Federal environmental review law (NEPA) and its state equivalents require comment periods before major actions. A comment becomes part of the legal record.

## Choosing

No choice has to be perfect. The first steps that cost least and do most are nearly always the same: **a written record** (a witness report and a records request), sent to **the body whose task it is** (an oversight office or the matching agency). That record strengthens every later step, including a conversation with a lawyer.

A witness report, or a complaint to an unrelated office, does not necessarily preserve the right to a legal remedy. Each process has its own deadline and filing requirements; they are checked for the specific process intended, and legal advice is sought early where it is needed.
