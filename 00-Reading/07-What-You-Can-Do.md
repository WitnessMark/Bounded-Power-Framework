# What You Can Do — A Plain-Language Guide to Your Options

*If an authority — a government office, an agency, a company, an institution — is doing something that harms you or people around you, you have more options than most people realize. This guide explains the common ones in plain language: what each one **is**, **when** it fits, **how to start**, and **what to expect**. A longer list is in the [Stop-Path and Protections Reference](../05-Reference/03-Analytical-Standards/Stop-Path-Protections-Reference.md).*

> This is general information to help you understand your options — **not legal advice**, and not a guarantee of any outcome. The options below are mostly under US law. Rules and deadlines vary by state and change over time. For anything serious, a short consult with a lawyer or a local legal-aid office is worth it. Many of these options have **deadlines**, so don't sit on a strong concern.
>
> **This framework does not replace any of these options.** No institution has adopted the Core Requirements, so using this framework does not by itself pause, stop, or change anything. Your rights under law still apply in full (Core Requirements 2.1).

---

## Start here: three questions

1. **Do you mainly want it written down and on the record?** → Write a **witness report** (this framework) and/or make a **public-records request**.
2. **Do you want an official body to look into it?** → File a **complaint** with an oversight office (an inspector general, an ombudsman, or the relevant agency).
3. **Were you personally harmed and want a remedy?** → Look at **administrative complaints**, **small claims**, or talk to a lawyer about a **civil-rights or civil suit**.

You can do several at once. Writing it down (1) costs little and strengthens everything else.

## The options

### 1. Public-records request (FOIA)
**What it is:** Your legal right to ask a government body for its records (emails, reports, data, policies). The federal law is the Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA"); states have their own public-records laws.
**When it fits:** You suspect something but need proof, or you want to see what an authority actually did or decided.
**How to start:** Find the agency's "public records" or "FOIA" page and send a written request describing the records you want. A short email can be enough.
**What to expect:** A response within a set number of days (it varies). Some records may be withheld, with a reason you can challenge. Often free or low-cost.

### 2. Write a witness report (this framework)
**What it is:** A structured, dated account of what an institution did, failed to do, or threatened to do — see the [Witness Report Filing Guide](09-Citizen-Witness-Report-Filing-Guide.md), or let an AI interview you with the [AI Witness Interview Bundle](10-AI-Witness-Interview-Bundle.md).
**When it fits:** You want a clear, dated, organized record of what happened, especially when the institution's own complaint channel goes nowhere. It also makes later steps easier: a complaint, a records request, or a talk with a lawyer.
**How to start:** Follow the filing guide, or the AI interview. Use public sources and your own observation only. Refer to people by role ("the caseworker"), not by name. Write UNKNOWN for anything you don't know.
**What to expect:** **The report is yours.** v1 of this framework has no place to file it, and writing it does not start any official process. You decide whether to keep it, share it, or attach it to a complaint. If you publish it, publishing is your choice and your responsibility. See [TERMS](../TERMS.md).

### 3. Complaint to an oversight body (inspector general, ombudsman, auditor)
**What it is:** Many agencies have an internal watchdog (an inspector general) or an independent ombudsman whose job is to investigate misconduct, waste, or unfair treatment.
**When it fits:** Misuse of authority, waste, retaliation, or a pattern you think an investigator should examine.
**How to start:** Search "[agency name] inspector general complaint" or "[your state] ombudsman". Most have an online complaint form.
**What to expect:** They may investigate, refer it, or decline, and they can often keep your identity confidential. No cost.

### 4. Agency administrative complaint (the specialized channels)
**What it is:** Specific harms have specific complaint systems — for example, discrimination (federal and state civil-rights offices), housing (the federal housing department), workplace safety (the federal workplace-safety agency), and consumer problems (your state attorney general or the federal consumer financial regulator).
**When it fits:** Your situation matches a category one of these offices handles.
**How to start:** Search "[type of harm] complaint [your state]" — for example, "housing discrimination complaint [your state]".
**What to expect:** An intake process, and possibly an investigation or mediation. These often have **strict deadlines** (sometimes 180 days), so act promptly. Usually free.

### 5. Your elected representatives, public comment, open meetings
**What it is:** Council members, state legislators, and members of Congress have staff who do "casework" — helping people who are stuck with an agency. Public bodies must also let you speak at meetings and comment on proposed rules.
**When it fits:** You're being stonewalled, or you want to influence a decision before it's final.
**How to start:** Contact your representative's office (most have a help form), or attend or comment at the relevant public meeting.
**What to expect:** Casework can unstick things quietly and quickly. Public comment creates a record that decision-makers must consider. Free.

### 6. Small claims court
**What it is:** A simplified court for modest money disputes, designed to be used **without a lawyer**.
**When it fits:** You suffered a specific, limited financial harm.
**How to start:** Your county court's website has a "small claims" section with the dollar limit and forms.
**What to expect:** A low filing fee, a fairly quick hearing, and you present your own case.

### 7. Civil-rights claim or civil lawsuit (with a lawyer)
**What it is:** If a government official violated your constitutional rights, a federal civil-rights law (42 U.S.C. § 1983) lets you sue. Other harms may support an ordinary civil suit.
**When it fits:** Serious harm — wrongful detention, excessive force, denial of due process, discrimination.
**How to start:** Talk to a civil-rights lawyer or a legal-aid office. Many offer free consultations and take strong cases on contingency (no upfront fee).
**What to expect:** Slower and more involved, but with real remedies (damages, court orders). **Deadlines apply** — don't wait.

### 8. Class action
**What it is:** One lawsuit on behalf of many people harmed the same way.
**When it fits:** The harm is small per person but affects many people.
**How to start:** A class-action firm or a legal-aid organization can evaluate it.
**What to expect:** It takes a long time, but it is how widespread harms get addressed when no single person's loss justifies a solo suit.

### 9. Whistleblower protections and the False Claims Act (if you're an insider)
**What it is:** Special protections — and sometimes financial rewards — for people who report fraud or wrongdoing from inside an organization. The False Claims Act ("qui tam") lets insiders sue over fraud against the government.
**When it fits:** You have inside knowledge of fraud, waste, or illegal conduct.
**How to start:** Talk to a whistleblower lawyer *before* you act — how you report matters for your protection.
**What to expect:** Strong protection against retaliation when done correctly.

### 10. If you get sued for speaking up: anti-SLAPP
**What it is:** Many states have "anti-SLAPP" laws that let you quickly throw out a lawsuit filed just to silence your public criticism, and recover your costs.
**When it fits:** An authority or company sues or threatens you over a good-faith public statement or report.
**How to start:** Tell a lawyer immediately that you think it's a SLAPP, and ask whether your state's anti-SLAPP law applies.
**What to expect:** A fast-track dismissal and possible recovery of fees, where it applies.

## If the harmed person is a child

- **Child protective services** — anyone can report suspected abuse or neglect to child protective services or a child-abuse hotline; some people are legally required to.
- **Special education and school records** — under federal law (IDEA and FERPA), children with disabilities have enforceable rights to services, and families can request records and dispute decisions.
- **Title IX** — protects against sex discrimination and harassment in schools.
- **Guardian ad litem or court-appointed advocate** — courts can appoint an independent advocate for a child's interests.

## If the harm is to the environment

- **Citizen suits** — most major federal environmental laws (the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act, and others) let **ordinary people sue** a violator, or a government agency that isn't enforcing the law. This is one of the most powerful and least-known tools.
- **Environmental agency complaints** — report pollution or permit violations to the federal or state environmental agency.
- **Public comment on permits and projects** — federal environmental review law (NEPA) and state equivalents require comment periods before major actions; your comment becomes part of the legal record.

## A note on choosing

You don't have to pick perfectly. The low-cost, high-value first moves are almost always: **write it down** (a witness report and a records request) and **send it to the body whose job it is** (an oversight office or the matching agency). That creates a paper trail that makes every later step — including talking to a lawyer — stronger. And remember the deadlines: when in doubt, start the clock by filing *something* now.
